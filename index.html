Context

A product everyone loves and almost no one pays for

Crafto lets users blend their photo with quotes, shayari and festival greetings, then share to WhatsApp. It's the #1 Art & Design app in India — 120M+ downloads, 4.58★, 50+ festivals a year driving organic re-engagement.

But premium is structured completely differently on each platform: Android runs a ₹2 trial that auto-converts to ₹199/month; iOS charges ₹99/month for a stripped-down version with no trial. Two platforms, two different ways of failing.

The Problem

Under 5% convert — the ones who do leave in 2–3 months

I scanned 1,774 reviews across both stores and ran a WTP survey (n=50). Not one 1-star review complained about content quality. Every payment complaint clustered on the same words — fraud, autopay, refund.

  • Android — trust problem. ₹2 trial silently fires ₹199 the next day. Rage-churn follows.
  • iOS — conversion problem. A ₹99 wall hits before anyone feels premium value. No trial, no proof.
  • Both — pricing gap. 78% of users have WTP ≤ ₹49/month. No tier exists for them.
Who's affected

Four users, four different jobs

Sunita · Indore

Sends Good Morning greetings daily. WTP ₹29–49/mo.
Ramu · Varanasi

Arun · Gwalior

Adds logo to promo posters. WTP ₹99–199/mo.
Priya · Pune
iOS · Metro professional
Wants branded content — no trial, no Hindi on iOS. WTP ₹99/mo.
Approach

Across the funnel, not just the price tag

My first instinct was pricing. But the data showed 96% of trial users never activate or retain — which no price change solves. So I scored solutions with RICE across six stages: acquisition, activation, retention, value, monetisation and trust. That reframing changed the winners.

The Solution

Three pillars, not two price fixes

01 · Foundation
Transparent consent & one-tap cancel. Personalised onboarding so users reach value fast. Unlocks everything else.
02 · Pricing
Android ₹29 Lite + AdMob nudge. iOS 7-day trial → Business Premium at ₹599/yr. Matches the WTP ladder.
03 · Moat
Vernacular AI festival greetings competitors can't copy, plus a "Made with Crafto" viral loop compounding the 50-festival cycle.
Reflection

What I'd do differently

My first cut leaned entirely on pricing. The data had been pointing past price all along — the activation gap and the under-used viral loop were bigger, cheaper wins. The strongest idea, vernacular AI greetings, was the one I originally ranked lowest. Scoping it to one tight use case made it viable and moved it to the top of the RICE table.

Uber Reserve: from problem definition to product improvement

Reserve adoption is low among Tier-1 urban professionals. I stripped the surface explanations to find the root truths — then designed three targeted fixes.

Role
Product discovery
Type
Product teardown
Lens
First principles · JTBD
Market
Tier-1 India
Context

Reserve exists — so why won't professionals use it?

Uber Reserve lets users pre-book a ride. Adoption stays low among Tier-1 urban professionals. Rather than jump to features, I set out to understand the problem from basic user needs — strip the assumptions, find the root truths, then talk solutions.

First Principles

Three root causes, not one

Reliability breaks trust
Early/late pickups, inconsistent drivers. When ad-hoc Uber already works, even small failures break trust completely.
Scheduling is rigid
Reserve demands a fixed pickup time — but urban professionals' plans shift constantly. Flexibility is a basic need.
Effort unjustified
Pre-booking asks for more planning and cost without a clearly better outcome than just opening the app.
Problem Statement

The synthesised problem

Reserve adoption is low because it doesn't feel clearly better or more reliable than normal booking. These users value reliability, flexibility and low mental effort — but Reserve's failures break trust rather than build certainty, its fixed time fights changing workdays, and its extra planning and cost don't feel worth the trade-off.

Jobs To Be Done

What users are actually hiring a ride for

  • Certainty for what matters. When I have an important meeting or flight, I want to trust my cab arrives on time so I can focus, not worry about delays.
  • Flexibility without penalty. When I don't know when my day ends, I want to book only when I'm ready, staying flexible if plans change.
  • Worth-it proof. When I consider paying extra or planning ahead, I want to clearly see the experience will be meaningfully better.
Research

What real users said

"Not more reliable than booking yourself immediately."— Paraphrased from user reviews and community threads

Several described Reserve as roughly a coin-flip. Others noted a reservation behaves no differently from booking 15 minutes ahead, since drivers aren't committed in advance.

Product Improvement

Three ideas, each aimed at a root cause

  • Driver commitment for Reserve rides. Once accepted, can't be cancelled casually — penalties for cancellation, rewards for completion. Targets the trust gap.
  • Flexible pickup window (15–20 min). Book a window, not an exact minute, easing stress when plans run late. Targets the rigidity gap.
  • Smart calendar nudges (opt-in). Detect flights or key meetings and prompt a Reserve booking at the right moment. Targets the effort gap.

Context

Meesho's growth problem isn't acquisition

Meesho is India's largest social commerce platform — ₹0 commission, COD-first, built for Tier-2/3 cities. It has scale. What it doesn't have is frequency. Most users shop once a month and a large proportion stop entirely after a few orders. Growing ARPU means getting existing users to order more and trust the platform enough to increase basket size.

The Problem

Three compounding barriers blocking growth

  • Trust gap. Product photos routinely don't match reality — users rate quality 1–3 out of 5. The moment expectations are violated, basket size shrinks and repeat purchase drops.
  • Discovery friction. Irrelevant, repetitive search results lead to session drop-offs before users find what they need. The platform has inventory; users can't find it.
  • Checkout deterrence. Late delivery fees and COD charges surfaced only at checkout cause abandonment and suppress ARPU — especially for multi-item orders where the economics look worse.
"Photos look very good on the app, but when the product arrives, it looks different or feels cheap."— Paraphrased from qualitative user interviews
Who's affected

Three distinct user segments, three different problems

Radha · Tier-3 town
Bargain Hunter · Age 28
Seeks lowest prices but avoids adding items due to delivery charges and COD fees — checkout economics suppress her basket.
Ajay · Tier-2 city
Discovery Seeker · Age 35
Wants variety but frustrated by irrelevant search results — he abandons sessions without finding what he came for.
Sita · Tier-2 city
Trust-Cautious Repeater · Age 32
Has bought before but hesitates to increase basket — quality mismatches once mean smaller orders forever.
Prioritisation

Six solutions scored on RICE — two clear winners

I scored six solutions on Reach × Impact × Confidence ÷ Effort. The top two had the highest scores because they attack the broadest problems with the highest confidence and manageable effort:

Free COD for Premium & Bundles · RICE 6.75
Remove checkout friction specifically for trusted products and multi-item orders. Highest reach, high confidence — directly attacks the suppressed ARPU problem for Radha and Sita.
Meesho Gold White-Label · RICE 3.20
Quality-assured white-labelled products with verified photos and faster returns. Rebuilds trust at scale. Higher effort but addresses the root cause of the trust gap, not just a symptom.
Enhanced FBT with Savings · RICE 3.20
Curated "Frequently Bought Together" bundles surfacing delivery and COD savings nudge Radha into multi-item orders by making the economics visible before checkout.

What I chose not to build first: Search Relevance Overhaul scored 2.10 despite high reach because effort was 5 and confidence was only 0.7 — a major algorithm rewrite with uncertain outcome. It's Ajay's primary fix but it belongs in Phase 2 after quick-win trust gains reduce churn.

Reflection

What I'd do differently

The Free COD solution has the highest RICE score, but it's a cost centre — the unit economics only work if the order value and retention lift justify the COD subsidy. My biggest assumption is that Meesho can absorb the COD cost at the premium tier, which requires GMV mix data I was estimating. I'd want to run a small holdout test on a single cohort before committing to the rollout economics.

I'd also have pushed harder on the returns flow — users cite slow, uncertain returns as a barrier to trying new categories, but it didn't score high enough on RICE to make the top 3. In hindsight it might be the single highest-leverage trust unlock that doesn't require building new inventory.

Context

Zomato is the market leader — the growth lever is frequency, not acquisition

Zomato has scale. The meaningful growth question for an established market leader isn't user acquisition — it's moving existing users from ordering 2–3 times a month to ordering more often. Getting 8M occasional users from 1.2 to 2.0 orders/month is worth more than acquiring new users at current CAC.

I structured the CY2026 strategy around one North Star Metric — Monthly Orders Per User (MOPU), target 2.5 → 3.4 — and built four OKRs that ladder up to it, each with input metrics (what the product team controls), output metrics (user behaviour changes), and success criteria.

Funnel Analysis

Only 17 of 100 app opens reach delivery — the biggest drop is discovery

The funnel: 100 app opens → 92 see feed → 65 click a restaurant (−27%, biggest drop) → 48 view menu → 32 add to cart → 26 reach checkout → 22 attempt payment → 19 place order → 17 receive delivery.

The −27% drop at restaurant click is decision paralysis — too many restaurants, no guidance. That single stage is the highest-leverage intervention: fix it and you move 27 users further down a funnel that currently loses 83% of them.

−27% · Restaurant click
Too many options, no personalisation. "Top 3 For You" + AI feed relevance. Projected +13% orders.
−16% · Menu view
Menu confusion, high prices. "Most Ordered" surfacing + instant reorder button. Projected +6% orders.
−6% · Checkout
Price shock from surprise fees. Show fees upfront in cart. Projected +2% conversion.
OKR Framework

Four objectives, each with a distinct lever

  • O1 · Drive order frequency through personalised discovery. Key Result: MOPU 2.5 → 3.4. Initiatives: AI personalised feed, instant reorder, smart nudges, Gold deepening. Measuring: restaurant CTR 65→78%, reorder adoption rate 35%.
  • O2 · Eliminate checkout friction and payment failures. Key Result: App→order conversion 14% → 16.5%. Initiatives: transparent pricing, one-tap checkout, payment retry. Measuring: fee-related drop-offs −40%, checkout completion time −45s.
  • O3 · Build trust through reliable delivery. Key Result: delivery issue rate 12% → 8.4%, D30 retention +12%. Initiatives: proactive delay alerts, supply balancing, instant refunds. Measuring: "where's my order?" queries −40%.
  • O4 · Maximise new user retention through perfect onboarding. Key Result: D30 28% → 35%. Initiatives: First Order Excellence programme, lifecycle triggers, use-case education. Measuring: D7 repeat rate +20%.
Cohort Strategy

Different users need different interventions

New users · 2.5M/mo
Try once, forget
D0: perfect first order. D3: ₹100 off second. D7: show popular nearby. D30: winback offer. Target: D30 28%→35%.
Occasional users · 8M
1–2 orders/mo
Gold trial + lunch reminders + weekend brunch campaigns. Target: 1.2→2.0 orders/month.
Power users · 3.4M
Small issues compound
VIP support lane, early feature access, concierge support. Goal: keep 90%+ retention on this high-value cohort.
Discount hunters · 4M
Only order with deals
Migrate to Gold by showing annual savings. Target: 75%→50% discount-only orders.
Reflection

What I'd do differently

The MOPU 2.5→3.4 target (+36%) is ambitious. The OKR cascade is directionally right but the confidence intervals on each initiative's contribution to MOPU need A/B validation — I've assigned projected lifts (+13% from feed AI, +6% from reorder) that assume independence, when in practice these initiatives likely have compounding effects in both directions.

The biggest assumption I'd flag: the −27% drop at restaurant click is treated as decision paralysis and fixed with personalisation. It might partly be intentional browsing behaviour — users opening the app without a specific craving and deciding not to order. If that's true, personalisation helps but doesn't fully solve it, and the actual lever is occasion-based triggers (meal-time nudges, weather triggers, office day detection).

Context

A ₹3,450Cr market growing at 6.37% — with no dominant app-first player

The Indian pet grooming market is ₹3,450Cr in 2024, projected to reach ₹6,285Cr by 2033. Rising pet ownership and urban disposable income are driving demand. But the experience is fragmented — physical salons require pet travel, mobile services have no app presence, and at-home services treat grooming as a non-core add-on. There is no player with Zepto's combination of app infrastructure, trust, and last-mile reach.

Consumer Research

The DIY majority isn't price-sensitive — it's friction-sensitive

Consumer survey findings revealed the central insight: the majority of pet owners who groom at home aren't doing so because they prefer it or can't afford professional services. They're doing it because the current options are inconvenient.

  • 60% would prefer doorstep grooming if the price premium is ≤15% over salon prices.
  • 40% have never used professional grooming — not from price objection but from friction (travel stress for pets, inconvenience, trust concerns).
  • 0% expressed discomfort with a society-level van model — the concept tested cleanly.
  • Travel anxiety was the #1 stated pain point — pets get distressed during salon trips, which creates owner guilt and avoidance.
Competitive Landscape

Four players, four gaps — and Zepto plugs all of them

HUFT
Premium physical salons. Gap: fixed location requires pet travel — the exact problem users cite.
Flying Fur
Mobile grooming vans. Gap: no app booking, limited scale and consistency — can't serve at Zepto's volume.
Urban Company
At-home via freelancers. Gap: grooming is non-core, generic groomer supply — no specialisation or trust layer.
Blinkit / Instamart
Pet products only. Gap: no services at all — adjacency without execution.

Zepto's moat: app booking + delivery infrastructure + society-level trust + local density. None of the incumbents can replicate this combination without building what Zepto already has.

Product Strategy

GO — three product levers that make it work

"Know Your Groomer" trust layer
Display certifications, hygiene checklist and pet-handling approach before booking. Addresses the trust concern that stops 40% from trying professional grooming at all.
Society Trial Days
Low-commitment first exposure — a van visits a housing society on a fixed day. Removes the "try for the first time" barrier without requiring individual scheduling commitment.
Post-Groom Product Suggestions
Groomer-recommended products surfaced after each session. Converts service engagement into repeat e-commerce purchases — the flywheel Zepto's business model needs.

Why GO: TAM of ₹409Cr in grooming services + ₹1,900Cr+ including pet products across 5 Tier-1 cities. Demand is validated. Trust levers are operationally controllable. And 60% app-booking preference maps directly to Zepto's existing infrastructure.

Reflection

What I'd do differently

The GO recommendation is confident, but the hardest constraint I undermodelled is groomer supply. Building a pipeline of certified, reliable groomers who can operate society-level vans at scale is an operations problem, not a product problem — and it's the single point of failure that could break the trust layer the entire strategy depends on. I'd want to stress-test the supply-side build timeline before committing to launch sequencing.

I'd also revisit the pricing model — I assumed a ≤15% premium is acceptable at ₹29–49 for most segments, but the ₹409Cr TAM estimate doesn't fully account for the occasional-use segment (Tier-1 pet owners who might pay ₹500–800 per session but only twice a year). That's a different unit economics model worth exploring separately.
